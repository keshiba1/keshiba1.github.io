The simplest way to make an Indian kitchen safer and cleaner is to filter and reuse frying oil within two to three days, stop scrubbing non-stick pans, switch to steel chopping boards, and label lookalike jars. These everyday habits protect your food, your cookware, and your budget.

## How can I reuse leftover frying oil safely?

Whenever you deep fry or shallow fry, some oil is left over — and most people simply throw it away. Used within a day or two, that oil is safe to reuse; the problem is the food particles floating in it, which carry taste and flavour into your next dish if you don't clean them out.

Here is the method:

1. Set a fine sieve or strainer over an oil container. Match the container and strainer size to how much oil you have.
2. Press a thin paper napkin into the strainer so it takes the strainer's full shape and no oil spills over the side.
3. Pour all the used oil through the napkin.
4. Scrape the pan with a silicone spatula so every drop comes out — an ordinary spatula leaves oil stuck to the pan.
5. Let it filter. A small quantity filters quickly; a large quantity may need a few hours covered on the side.

What drips through is completely clear. Use it for a *dal* *tadka* or while making *sabzi*, and finish it within two to three days. Do not store frying oil for a long time.

## How do I clean a non-stick pan without damaging it?

Non-stick pans get ruined by scrubbing — the layers lift off and the pan is finished. Since these pans are costly and can't be replaced again and again, they deserve gentle handling.

Instead of scrubbing, put the pan back on the gas and pour water into it. Let the water boil and heat well. As it heats, the stuck gravy, food, and oil release into the water. Stir once with a wooden or silicone spatula, then pour the hot water out — the dirt comes away with it. Wash what remains gently with a soft brush or sponge. The same boil-and-soak method works for a non-stick *kadhai*, pan, or *tawa*.

One caution: if the pan is hot, let it cool on the gas or on the counter before washing, not straight under water.

## When should I throw a non-stick pan away?

Stop using any non-stick pan that has many scratches or a damaged base right now. Once the coating wears off, the chemicals in it can enter your food, and the pan is no longer safe. A non-stick pan is only safe while its surface is intact.

To keep it that way, use a good soft brush or sponge and only silicone or wooden spatulas — never anything that scrapes.

## Which chopping board is actually safe to use daily?

Stainless steel. Wooden chopping boards look natural but aren't safe over time: food lodges in the scratches and gaps you can't see, and stays there even after a thorough wash. Plastic boards have exactly the same problem — food sticks in the scratches and doesn't come out.

Steel is different. Even when it gets scratched, one wash cleans it completely; nothing stays stuck, and it doesn't hold smells after cutting fruit or vegetables. It comes in different sizes — a large board can be used both to knead dough and to cut vegetables and fruit, and it sits steady on the counter without sliding. Steel boards are also dishwasher-safe.

And skip the plastic spatulas that come bundled with chopping boards. They aren't cleaned fully by washing, and on heating they can release chemicals into food. Use a silicone spatula instead — safe for both baking and cooking.

## How should I store food in kitchen containers so it doesn't spoil?

Moisture is the enemy. Wet containers that aren't wiped properly will spoil whatever you put in them.

- Wash containers the moment they empty, so they have time to dry before the next refill.
- For jars that aren't very dirty — a tea *dabba*, for example — wipe thoroughly with a dry napkin instead of washing.
- Always wipe a container dry before refilling it, whether it holds tea leaves, sugar, or *namak*.
- Match the jar to the quantity. Don't store a small amount in a big container; use small jars for small quantities.

## How do I keep foil and cling film clean when they won't fit back in the box?

Boxes of aluminium foil and cling film tear easily, so the rolls end up sitting out in the open — collecting dust that then touches your food when you wrap it. Two low-cost fixes keep them clean:

- Slip the rolls into a small cloth shopping bag and tie it shut so no dust gets in. No bag? Cut the sides of a large shopping bag and stitch a smaller one.
- Or wrap each roll in butter paper, roll it up, and tuck the extra paper in from both ends. Butter paper doesn't tear quickly, so one wrap lasts a long time.

## What are some zero-cost tricks that make a kitchen easier to run?

Oil dispensers leave stains wherever they stand. Save the lid from any broken bottle, or use a small plate, and stand the oil bottle on it — the rack surface stays clean and you skip repeated wiping. Lids from takeaway boxes work the same way, and they're free.

For lookalike jars, labeling saves real frustration. Instant coffee and filter coffee look identical, and cocoa powder is the same colour — without labels, anyone else in the kitchen has to open and smell each one. Write the name on each lid with a permanent marker, or stick cheap white tape on the jar and label it.

And if you often carry a cake outside, a locking cake holder makes it easy — it holds firm, nothing falls out, and the same holder stores fruit and vegetables at home.
