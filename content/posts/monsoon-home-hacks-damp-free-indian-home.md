The key to a fresh home in the monsoon is controlling moisture everywhere it hides — on your bedsheets, in your wardrobe, on towels, and inside snack packets — using airflow, camphor, and weekly cleaning.

The rainy season is refreshing, but humidity brings a distinct damp smell, slow-drying laundry, and germs that thrive in the moisture. Here are 13 practical hacks to keep your home fresh and your work easier through the monsoon.

## How do I keep bedsheets and pillow covers germ-free in the monsoon?

In the monsoon, your bedsheets, pillow covers and cushion covers stay slightly damp because of the humidity in the air, and germs get an ideal place to settle. In other seasons you can change these every 10–12 days, but during the rains, change them **every week**. You sleep, sit, and rest your face directly on these, so dusting alone won't clean them — a fresh sheet each week protects you from germs and bacteria.

## How can I use camphor to keep my home fresh?

Camphor is the monsoon workhorse in this routine. Whenever you take out an old bedsheet, burn a little camphor in the bedroom and let the smoke spread through the house — it kills germs and leaves a refreshing fragrance.

Burning camphor directly is risky near curtains and other flammable things, so do it carefully and attentively. The safer option is an **electrical diffuser** with no open flame. These plug into any corner, aren't very costly, and you can buy two or three. Add camphor on top — you can also add flowers, cloves, cardamom, coffee, or an essential oil of your choice — and leave it on in any corner for two to three hours.

## Where should I place camphor potlis around the house?

Inside enclosed spaces you can't run a diffuser, so make a small *potli* (pouch) by wrapping camphor in a napkin:

1. Place one potli inside your wardrobe, under clothes or in a corner, to stop the damp smell from getting into your clothes.
2. Put one at the bottom of your dustbin, under the garbage bag, so you get camphor's fragrance instead of the smell of leftover food.

Wooden wardrobes absorb moisture quickly, so the potli keeps both the wardrobe and its contents smelling fresh.

## How do I dry towels and mats so they don't smell?

Wet towels struggle to dry if you throw them anywhere or leave them hanging in a poorly ventilated bathroom — they need airflow. A drying rack works well: you can dry two or three towels on it with gaps between them for proper airflow, and it fits inside a room, which dries things faster than a rain-hit balcony.

No stand? Hang towels on a hanger anywhere in the room and turn the fan on full speed. Always spread a towel out fully — a bunched-up towel stays wet all day, smells, and becomes unhygienic. The mats near the bathroom and kitchen sink also dry slowly, so keep changing them and try to keep them dry.

## How should I dry clothes indoors during the monsoon?

Open-air clothes barely dry in the rains, and sudden showers re-wet half-dried laundry. Dry clothes **inside under a fan on full speed**, spacing each piece apart so air reaches it. A few habits make this easier:

1. Wash slightly fewer clothes each day so you need less drying space.
2. Dry with distance between pieces for faster airflow.
3. Check that clothes are fully dry before folding — half-dry clothes carry a smell everyone notices, and only re-washing removes it.
4. Once a batch dries well, wash the next batch.

Washing fewer clothes at a time also cuts your workload, since folding stays pending until everything is properly dry.

## Why should furniture and walls get more attention now?

Touch a wall in the monsoon and you'll feel moisture. Don't keep furniture — wooden or otherwise — pressed directly against the wall. Leave a little gap so air circulates and wall dampness doesn't transfer, keeping wooden furniture especially safe from moisture.

Cross ventilation matters just as much. Keep windows open so air flows through the whole house; damp walls get a better chance to dry, and your furniture and rooms stay fresh.

## Which chopping board is best for the rainy season?

A **stainless steel chopping board** suits every season and is especially good for the monsoon, when you want to avoid germs. Steel doesn't transfer any food smell, washes easily with soap and a brush, and doesn't scratch. Despite the assumption that steel boards are heavy, this one is lightweight and thin, so it's easy to handle, hang or store — a hygienic surface where germs don't settle.

## How can I stop biscuits and snacks from turning soft?

Every monsoon, open biscuit and snack packets go soft because moisture creeps in — and airtight boxes alone don't help. Two fixes:

1. **Paper cover:** Lay a sheet of paper (or newspaper) directly over the open packets before closing the box. No air and no moisture get in, so the packets don't spoil.
2. **Foil-seal with an iron:** Cover the packet's open end neatly with aluminium foil and flatten it. Press a well-heated iron on top, and the whole open portion seals shut — just like a market-fresh pack, no gaps, no box needed. Re-seal it this way each time you open it.

These 13 hacks tackle the monsoon's real enemy — trapped moisture — across your bedroom, wardrobe, bathroom, laundry and kitchen, keeping your home fresh and your daily work lighter.
