The cheapest way to organize your kitchen is to stop shopping and start reusing — nearly everything you need is already sitting unused in your home.

Before you spend a rupee on market organizers, look at what you already own. Parcel boxes, empty glass jars, old buckets, worn plastic boxes, bangles you no longer wear, and leftover cloth can all do the job. Here are my reuse-first ideas, grouped by where they solve a problem.

## How do I organize my fridge using boxes I already have?

The boxes your online parcels arrive in are sturdier than they look and reusable for a good while. Instead of throwing them away, I put them to work inside the fridge:

1. **Chopped vegetables.** If I'm making a mixed-vegetable dish the next day, I wash and chop everything the night before and store it in a box. Green vegetables especially stay fine for a day or two this way.
2. **Dairy and aromatics.** I keep one box for butter packets, cheese cubes or cheese slices; another for ginger, garlic, green chillies and coriander.
3. **Leftovers.** Small worn plastic boxes that would otherwise be thrown out are perfect for storing leftovers.

The payoff is visibility. Because these boxes are transparent on top, I can see what's inside at a glance, and my vegetable basket stops looking messy with loose produce scattered around. A tidy fridge is mostly about giving small things a fixed place.

## What household items can replace store-bought kitchen storage?

Before buying fancy bottles from the market, check what things you already bought came packed in. You'll often find genuinely nice glass bottles — the ones with white lids that once held ginger-garlic paste, for example. I collect a few and use them for small grocery items or *masala*. There's no need to spend money on new ones.

The same logic applies across the kitchen:

- **Old small buckets** that don't get used: take any cloth or paper shopping bag, or even old newspaper, and flip it over the bucket to cover it completely. It stops looking like an old bucket at all and becomes a storage unit for big wooden spatulas, *belan*, or kitchen towels.
- **Colourful kids' glasses** — the cartoon-character ones I bought for my kids that have since grown up — look lovely on a kitchen shelf and are perfect for holding small daily-use items. If you ever felt you wasted money on them, this is how you get the value back.
- **Worn plastic boxes** hold my counter-wiping and dusting cloths, or extra stock of dish soap, laundry soap, hand soap and small pouches.
- **Old bangles** I love but no longer wear — even slightly out-of-shape ones — hang kitchen towels beautifully and add a bit of colour.

The point isn't decoration for its own sake. Reusing items like this gives a lot of loose things a fixed place, keeps my kitchen organized, and doesn't cost a rupee.

## How do I protect a wooden rack from oil and ghee stains for free?

Oil bottles and *ghee* tins leave a stain no matter how carefully you wipe them, and you can't keep washing a wooden surface with water again and again. My fix is a barrier I already have. A lot of paper bags come in fairly thick paper with a nice design — I cut one to the size of my rack and place my oil bottles on top. The bottles never touch the wood directly, and the rack stays clean without repeated washing. A plastic mat works too, but the paper-bag version is free.

## What's the best way to hide an extra gas cylinder?

Most kitchens keep a spare cylinder in case the one in use runs out, but not every kitchen has a dedicated spot for it, so it ends up out in the open. I cover mine with any nice printed cloth — an old *dupatta* piece, leftover fabric from a kurta, anything on hand. You don't need to stitch it: wrap it around and tuck the edges in underneath. Once covered, no one can tell there's a cylinder there, and a printed fabric looks even better. I set a small plate or tray on top and use it to hold a basket of potatoes and onions — the dead space becomes extra storage.

## How do I keep my dustbin from getting filthy?

Throwing dry waste directly into the bin is what makes it dirty over time. Instead, I line it with a cloth shopping bag — you'll have plenty from grocery runs, and since it only holds dry waste, it won't get wet or damaged. To keep the bag from slipping:

1. Stick one adhesive hook on each inside wall of the bin, directly opposite each other. They don't even need to be strong hooks.
2. Hang the bag — plastic or cloth — over both hooks.
3. Toss waste in; the bag stays put and doesn't move around.

This is ideal if you dislike using plastic garbage bags, because a cloth bag works just as well for dry waste.

## Should I paint tired furniture instead of replacing it?

When a wardrobe or wooden piece has worn out but I can't replace it right away, a fresh coat of colour gives it a new look. I cover it fully in white first — once something is completely white, any other colour goes on top far more smoothly, because painting directly over a different base colour means the new colour doesn't take properly. From there I mix in a little black for grey shades, keeping some drawers light grey and some dark. Oil paint gives the most polished finish on wood, but I'm allergic to it, so I use wall paint instead — it looks a little rough after one coat but improves a lot after a second. A few small stickers add character. It's not strictly kitchen work, but the same refresh-don't-replace mindset runs through every idea here.

Every hack above works in any room of the home, not just the kitchen — the common thread is looking at what you already own before you buy anything new.
