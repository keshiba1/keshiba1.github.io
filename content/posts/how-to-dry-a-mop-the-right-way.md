The house smell that lingers after mopping isn't dirt you missed — it's a wet, poorly dried mop. Wash it properly after every use and dry it the right way, and that smell disappears for good.

This post covers the correct mop-cleaning and drying routine, plus a few more low-cost Indian kitchen hacks: airtight storage for spices and vegetables, a soap dish that stops wastage, and two market-style sweets you can make at home.

## Why does my house smell after mopping, and how do I fix it?

After mopping, the floor looks clean but a strange, unpleasant smell can fill the house. That smell comes from a mop that isn't cleaned or dried properly. A wet, dirty mop used across the whole house spreads dirt in ways you can't see, and the odour lingers so long that no perfume, room spray or incense will remove it.

Here's the routine that keeps a mop clean and smell-free:

1. After every mopping session, wash the mop thoroughly with clean water until all the dirt comes out.
2. Add a little detergent so it gets properly clean — a rotating mop cleans easily in its tub or bucket; a plain cloth should be washed with soap by hand.
3. Squeeze out all the excess water.
4. Bend the mop so the head opens upward, then hang it on a hook where it stays spread open and gets airflow.
5. Dry regular mopping cloths outside so they don't smell either.

## How long does it take for a mop to dry properly?

With good ventilation — and a fan running if needed — a mop dries in three to four hours, and always within the same day. Left lying flat on the floor, it won't dry even in two days and will start to smell. Wash it every time and the mop head stays white and clean for a long time.

## How do I store spices so they don't spoil in the rainy season?

Most spice boxes have lids that don't close tightly, letting moisture in — and in the rainy season that moisture causes fungus and wastes a lot of food. The fix is an airtight box that locks from all four sides so no air gets in.

A good spice organizer holds eight separate small boxes inside for eight different spices, whether powdered or whole. Look for these features:

1. A tight lock on all four sides so no air reaches the *masala* inside.
2. A lid with a built-in handle so you can carry it without it falling.
3. Enough gap between the inner boxes to fit a big spoon, or room for small spoons inside — many spice boxes skip this, which is exactly why their lids never close fully.

Because it's airtight and easy to carry, the spices won't mix, won't catch moisture, and won't spill if you're moving in a hurry. The same box is multi-purpose — you can use it in the fridge for small or cut vegetables, or even in a wardrobe or on a dressing table.

## How can I organize cut vegetables and herbs in the fridge?

Use a similar airtight box with inner baskets for the fridge. The lid has a holder and locks on all four sides, so if you store cut onions or garlic, the smell won't spread into the fridge. The inner baskets have tiny holes that keep vegetables fresh.

Keep the three or four things you always need while cooking *sabzi* or *dal* together in one box — curry leaves, green chillies and coriander leaves. Then whenever you cook, you pick up one box from the fridge and have everything in hand. A fourth compartment can hold ginger and garlic. If you don't want the inner baskets, use the whole container as one airtight box for cake, sweets, or anything that needs to stay fresh.

## How do I stop soap from melting and getting wasted near the sink?

Soap kept near the kitchen sink or wash basin gets wet, melts fast and finishes quickly. Ordinary flat soap dishes make it worse because water collects in them and the soap sits dissolving.

Switch to a soap dish with holes and a tray underneath. The extra water drains through the holes into the tray below, so the soap stays drier and lasts far longer. After using the soap, check back in a while, remove the water settled at the bottom, and put the soap back so it dries faster. These come in different styles — including a butterfly-shaped one that looks nice — but the principle is the same: let the water settle below and the soap dry on top. Less wastage means less money wasted too.

## Why are there white stains on my utensils after washing?

White stains don't mean the utensil is dirty — they mean it wasn't rinsed properly after soap was applied. The stain is just leftover soap residue.

If you keep seeing white marks, keep a clean cloth aside for washing utensils: after applying soap, wipe the utensil with that cloth to remove any residue, or rinse thoroughly under running water by hand for a good while. This matters for health, not just looks — soap residue on utensils passes into your food and into your stomach.

## Can I make jalebi and laddu at home instead of buying them?

Yes — and homemade means fresh, hygienic, and noticeably better tasting.

For **jalebi without maida**, mix equal quantities of besan (gram flour) and rice flour — one cup each — with one teaspoon of baking powder and enough water to make a slightly thick batter (too thin and it won't form). Rest it for half an hour. Fry on a flat pan, not a *kadhai*, using just a little oil. Fill an icing cone with the batter, make a very small hole, and shape the jalebis. Meanwhile prepare a light sugar syrup — for two bowls of sugar, one to one-and-a-half bowls of water — boiled until slightly thick, then cooled. Dip fried jalebis only in cooled syrup, because hot syrup makes them go soft. Lift them out with a slotted spoon once the next batch is done so they don't over-absorb and turn soggy.

Don't throw away leftover syrup (from jalebi, rasgulla or gulab jamun). Add plain ready-made boondi, one to one-and-a-half teaspoons of cardamom powder, and dry fruits or raisins if you like. Cover and let the boondi fully absorb the syrup, add a little orange food colour to match the market look, mash lightly so it holds, and shape the laddus. Full recipes are in the video description.
