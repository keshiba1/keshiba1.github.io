If any kitchen task feels harder than it should, the fix usually starts with what you're using for the job and how you've arranged it — the right scrubber, spoon, or storage box quietly saves you effort every single day.

## Which scrubber should I use to wash dishes?

The dishwashing liquid matters, but the scrubber matters even more — it needs to be gentle on your hands, clean dishes well, and last a long time. The single most important thing to check is whether the scrubber has wire mesh built into it. Many do, and that wire kind irritates skin over time. Worse, the wire strands sometimes come loose and stick to dishes — not something you want anywhere near your food.

A completely plain scrubber solves both problems. I use a plain three-pack from IKEA for around ₹125. I also tried cheaper scrubbers from Amazon — a pack of 8-10 for around ₹200-250 — which were decent on the hands with no rashes, but went soft and the foam inside broke down much faster, wearing out within about 15 days of regular use. The IKEA pack held up noticeably better. There's a price difference, but spending a bit more upfront on something that lasts is usually worth it.

## How should I store ceramic bowls so they don't break?

Ceramic bowls are handy for soup, *khichdi*, or any semi-liquid food, and they're useful for serving when guests are over. But ceramic chips or breaks easily if it isn't handled carefully, so storage matters.

Keep them in a transparent box. This does three things at once: you don't have to hunt for pieces, everything stays dust-free, and it's all stored safely together. Add any older small ceramic bowls you already own into the same box so they're consolidated in one place.

## How do I keep salt dry during the monsoon?

Storing salt properly during the monsoon is genuinely tricky — moisture creeps in no matter where you keep it. Two things decide whether your salt clumps: the container and the spoon.

1. Keep salt in a small ceramic bowl, the kind usually used for pickles and *achaar*.
2. Never leave a steel spoon sitting inside the salt — steel draws in moisture and makes salt clump and turn damp fast.
3. Use a plastic or wooden spoon instead.
4. Store that spoon separately from the salt, in its own little bowl or glass.
5. Dip the spoon in only when you actually need salt, then take it straight back out.

Every extra minute a spoon or extra air sits inside the pot, more moisture gets in and the faster the salt spoils. The discipline of removing the spoon each time is the whole trick.

## How can I stop container lids from going missing?

Lids disappearing is one of the most common kitchen problems, and if you don't keep lids and containers together, it also eats up shelf space you don't have in a small kitchen.

The fix is simply to gather all your lids in one dedicated spot. I kept a small gift box that came with a built-in divider instead of throwing it away, and now store lids on one side of it. You don't need anything fancy — the options include:

- A basket with a divider
- Any old cardboard box
- A larger transparent container, so everything is visible in one place
- Free food-delivery containers you'd otherwise discard

The point is keeping lids and containers together so the little rubber seals or the lids themselves never go missing when you actually need to cover something. If your kitchen trolley has spare space, use it — stack containers on one side and tuck lids into the leftover space next to them, right by the mixer jars, since these get reached for daily for leftovers.

## Why should I vacuum before mopping?

Keeping the house clean daily takes real time and energy, but it's a task you can't skip. If you sweep and mop yourself, a good vacuum cleaner is the kind of investment that keeps paying off for years.

The direct answer: run the vacuum over the whole house before you mop, and you barely need to sweep at all. Kids create a lot of mess, the kitchen especially — vegetable scraps, onion peels, and hair that a broom just doesn't pick up well. Vacuuming first collects all of it in one quick pass, without the bending, without the dust a broom kicks up, and without bits left behind in the dustpan.

There's a second benefit for your mop. Once the floor is vacuumed, mopping becomes so much easier — no hair or debris sticking to the mop, which keeps the mop itself cleaner and much easier to wash. If a mop only picks up dust instead of hair and debris, maintaining it is genuinely simple; otherwise it wears out fast and takes forever to clean. Since sweeping and picking up debris is usually the slowest part of cleaning, vacuuming first gets the whole house clean in minutes.

## What else can a vacuum cleaner do around the house?

A vacuum earns its cost by doing far more than floors:

- **Furniture and sofas** — most vacuums come with attachments; a pass once or twice a week keeps them from getting grimy.
- **The car** — especially small bits of debris stuck in car carpets that are otherwise a pain to clean. I use a wet-and-dry car vacuum with strong suction that works great for the house too.
- **Cobwebs** — suction them straight up instead of knocking them down and letting everything scatter across the floor, keeping both the floor and the air clean.
- **Walls, corners, and ceilings** — a vacuum is one of the easiest tools for these hard-to-reach spots.
