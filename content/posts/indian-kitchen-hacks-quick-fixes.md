The fastest way to fix the most common Indian kitchen problems is to keep a handful of small, low-cost tricks ready — from reviving old *idli* batter with Eno to storing extra utensils in transparent boxes in a non-modular kitchen.

Kitchen work takes up more time than almost any other household chore, so these hacks are built to make that work faster, cleaner, and less frustrating.

## How do I make soft idli from batter that has gone old?

If your *idli* batter is two or three days old, it stops rising and the *idli* comes out flat instead of soft and fluffy. The fix is Eno.

Add Eno according to the quantity of batter — for a full bowl, use one full packet. Use only the plain lemon Eno, never a flavoured one, because plain Eno adds no separate taste at all. You can use baking soda instead, but soda leaves a slight taste behind, which is why Eno is the better choice.

Batter mixed with Eno can still be stored. Use it again the next day, and you won't need to add Eno a second time.

## What is the fastest way to steam a batch of idlis?

A few small choices cut the cooking time in half and give you more *idli* per batch:

1. Use a bigger *idli* container. A larger steel container — like the Indus Valley one used here — holds bigger moulds, so one go makes at least 14 to 16 *idli* instead of forcing you to steam again and again.
2. Put the water on to heat while you fill the batter. When the water is already hot, cooking starts faster and the *idli* is ready in about 10 minutes instead of 20.
3. Fill the moulds generously. The thicker the *idli*, the softer it turns out; too little batter makes it thin and soggy.
4. Store finished *idli* in a roti casserole. It keeps them soft and warm for far longer than an ordinary container, where they cool down quickly.

## Why should I wash dal and rice until the water is clear?

Whatever grain you use — *dal* or rice — wash it until the water becomes completely transparent, not just two or three quick rinses.

Grains are stored in very large quantities, and things like anti-ant powder are mixed in during storage. If you don't wash these out fully, they can cause illness. So keep washing until the water runs completely clear.

## How do I cook dal without messing up the stove?

Cooking *dal* usually leaves the stove and countertop splashed with starchy water. Two steps prevent this.

First, keep the water quantity low — *dal* cooks easily even in a small amount of water, so there's less to boil over. Before putting the lid on, blow through it once to clear anything stuck inside, and clean the whistle well.

Second, use kitchen roll as a shield. Thick kitchen roll soaks up water easily. Fold it double, cut it in the middle, and place it over the cooker. Even if a little water escapes from the top, the paper absorbs it, so there are no splashes on the stove and your countertop stays clean.

## Can I sharpen knives and scissors at home?

Yes — you don't need to send them out. Over time scissors and knives go dull and cutting becomes difficult, but any cup fixes it.

Turn an old or new cup upside down and rub the blade against the base for two to three minutes. This sharpens both sides of a knife, and the same method works for scissors. It's simple enough that anyone can do it at home.

## How do I remove sticky sticker residue from utensils?

Sticker glue often stays behind even after the sticker peels off, and it won't budge with scrubbing, soap, hot water, or even a dishwasher.

The clean fix is a nail polish remover strip. These strips carry a natural oil that lifts the stickiness with just a little rubbing — no scrubbing needed — and they won't harm steel, plastic, ceramic, or glass. After rubbing, wipe the utensil with a wet cloth or wash it. Do not use liquid nail polish remover, which leaves stains on utensils.

## How do I store utensils in a non-modular kitchen?

In a kitchen with no trolleys, cabinets, or shelves, extra utensils and rarely used items are the hardest to place. Large boxes solve this.

Big items like an *idli* maker don't fit in trolleys and get spoiled if left out in the open — meaning you'd have to wash them again before use. Instead, store them inside a box that keeps them clean and ready. Some products, like the *idli* box itself, already come with a suitable box.

A few pointers make box storage work well:

1. Keep the boxes in the open space below the countertop, which is always available in a non-modular kitchen.
2. Choose transparent boxes where possible, so you can see the contents from the top and stop wasting time searching.
3. Label non-transparent boxes with a tag listing what's inside.
4. Pick a size to match your kitchen — the 60-litre box shown here was bought for ₹699, and larger and smaller sizes are available.

These boxes are dustproof and some come on wheels, so they move around easily. Check plastic shops in your city to find them cheaply. Even a modular kitchen can run short on space, so these boxes help there too, replacing items that otherwise sit on top and need repeated wiping.

## How do I keep roti and paratha soft in a tiffin?

Roti, *paratha*, and *dosa* packed in a *tiffin* often go dry and hard, and children can't eat them. Dryness is the cause, so seal the air out.

Wrap the item in cling film before packing. Food-grade cling film is made specifically for wrapping and storing food, so it's safe — but tell children to remove it fully before eating. If you prefer paper, butter paper works just as well: it's cheap, available in stationery shops, safe enough to use for baking, and keeps *roti*, *paratha*, or *dosa* soft and warm for a long time.
